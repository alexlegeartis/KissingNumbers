# 16D addendum: 52 old deletions necessary for a gain at any full Latin scaffold

This strengthens the signed cheap-trade result for the complete fixed quaternionic scaffold and any three full uniform-level Latin layers of [Takhanov, arXiv:2611.00927](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00927v1). No bound changed. Use the [main result](README.md) and its complete real-domain reduction to signed \(C^4\), exact costs (8 or 36), and cheap-slot load inequality \(m_i+2u_i\le4\). All blocker sets refer to this same **original** stock of 3,072 signed Latin-layer points and are counted as points, not orbits.

**Claim.** A profitable simultaneous replacement preserving the scaffold requires at least two cost-36 candidates and at least **52 distinct deletions from the old Latin layers**. It need not preserve any entire old Latin layer or use antipodal signs.

## A single expensive ray always spoils at least one cheap-saturation anchor

The old level of maximum multiplicity in an expensive candidate \(x\) is its doubled or majority level. Fix an old point \(z\) of that level that \(x\) blocks. As in the main result rotate each anchor letter to \(e_0\), preserving the common level relabeling. In unit block coordinates, a cheap switch changes one anchor axis; a cheap mixed row changes two slots to half-sign words with first coordinate positive. At a given slot their free 3-bit words are distinct, at most four in total. In a face with one bit fixed at most two can occur, by the same opposite-parity distance-one argument from the main result. These facts also hold for any subset of the cheap rows.

We show that in the presence of \(x\), a compatible old-anchor neighborhood has at most seven cheap rows:

1. **Doubled level (2,1,1).** At the two anchor-level positions both letters of \(x\) equal the anchor signed axes, and its two half-sign letters have positive first coefficient and different levels. On either half-sign slot \(j\), a mixed cheap row active there cannot use \(x_j\) itself if the same level; at the opposite level it must use the unique free-bit complement of \(x_j\). To see this, if the changed pair uses one anchor-level slot and \(j\), the other three slots already contribute two. If its two slots are both half-sign slots, the two unchanged levels already contribute two and exactly one changed slot has the same non-anchor level; its product is nonnegative, so the other must give \(-1/2\). Therefore with no switch at \(j\) there are at most three active mixed words: at most three in the same level if no opposite, and if both levels occur at most two same plus the single possible complement. A switch at \(j\) must have product \(-1/2\) with \(x_j\); it is unique up to the choice of alternative axis, and two opposite switches cannot both remain. It forces every active mixed word to share \(x_j\)'s bit on that axis. The complement becomes impossible and only one same-level word other than \(x_j\) fits the face. So \(m_j+2u_j\le3\) in both cases.

2. **Majority (3,1), all three majority letters equal the anchor.** If the remaining half sign has positive first coefficient, no switch can fit. Any mixed row must use the remaining slot, and there its word must be the unique opposite-level free-bit complement to produce \(-1/2\). In particular fewer than eight. If the remaining half sign has negative first coefficient, no switch can use that slot since the other three slots already contribute three. An active mixed word there must produce at most \(-1/2\). It is either the unique same-level antipode, or one of three opposite-level words at free-bit distance one from that antipode. If the same-level antipode occurs, the cheap-neighborhood uniqueness of a distance-one partner permits at most one from the other group. Thus the load in this slot is at most three, again a deficit.

3. **Majority (3,1), exactly two majority letters equal the anchor.** The third majority letter is a different signed axis \(a\perp e_0\) in its slot \(j\), and the singleton half sign in slot \(l\) has positive first coefficient (otherwise \(x\) would not block this anchor). The only possible cheap switch in \(j\) is \(-a\), since the other three coordinates contribute \(5/2\). For a mixed cheap row active at \(j\) but not \(l\), the other three coordinates contribute two, so its product with \(a\) must be \(-1/2\). A row active in both \(j,l\) whose product with \(a\) is \(+1/2\) must have product \(-1/2\) at \(l\), forcing the unique free-bit complement at \(l\). There is thus at most one positive-bit active word at \(j\); the negative-bit face has at most two. Without a switch the load is at most three. With switch \(-a\), all active words in \(j\) must have the positive bit, so \(m_j+2u_j\le1+2=3\).

A majority anchor cannot have any other pattern: to exceed two either it matches all three signed majority letters (the singleton sign can be either), or it matches two and is orthogonal on the third with positive singleton. In every case at least one slot has load \(\le3\) instead of four. Summation gives an integer cap seven. If a trade selected only one expensive candidate, choose any of its old doubled/majority blockers. With \(D\) old deletions and \(U\) cheap insertions, counting cheap blocker incidences gives \(8U\le8D-1\), so \(U+1\le D\). Thus **one expensive candidate cannot gain**, while zero was already ruled out.

## Two expensive rays have at most 20 common old blockers

Call the two expensive types \(A=(2,1,1)\) with one doubled level, and \(B=(3,1)\) with one majority and one singleton level. Use three observations which hold for every underlying Latin cube:

* A singleton blocker has one prescribed signed letter in its singleton slot, and exactly one sign choice per axis word. Two singleton conditions in different slots have at most four common axis words; at the same slot differing signed letters have none. If both singleton slots and letters agree, and the two candidates differ anywhere off that slot, their sign maps on the four axes of the old singleton level differ in at least one place: after rotating that level to the coordinate axes, the two other levels are precisely the two parities of half signs, and the four signs identify the actual letter. The two-axis Latin projection property makes each of those four possibilities occur exactly four times. Therefore at most 12 singleton blockers remain common in this case. If the differing off-slot letters are in the *same* level, they are orthogonal or antipodal, so at least two signs differ, hence overlap at most eight.
* A doubled blocker forces both doubled signed letters, and again has only one sign choice per underlying axis word. In particular, for two doubled sets of positions sharing just one slot the overlap in that level is at most one (three prescribed axes fix a Latin word).
* A majority blocker must match at least two of the three signed majority letters. A majority and a singleton condition in the same old level have at most seven common blockers: if the singleton slot lies outside the three majority slots there are at most three underlying axis words (pick two majority matches in addition to the singleton), if it imposes a different axis from the majority there then at most one, and if it matches a majority position and axis then at most \(4+4-1=7\) (at least one of two remaining majority axes). Each singleton word has only one sign choice.

The case accounting is as follows.

**Two type A.** If the doubled levels differ, there are at most four common blockers in each doubled level and at most 12 in the common singleton level, total 20. If doubled levels agree and both singleton positions move, use at most four in each singleton and four in the doubled level, total 12. If exactly one singleton position moves, the doubled positions share one slot, giving at most one, plus four on the moved singleton and 12 on the fixed singleton, total 17. If positions agree but a singleton signed letter differs, its overlap is zero, and \(4+16\le20\) suffices. If both singleton letters agree, a doubled letter differs, making zero overlap in the doubled layer and at most eight in each singleton (the differing off-slot letter is of the same level).

**One of each type.** If the doubled type A level occurs in type B, their shared level counts cap the overlap by \(4+16=20\). If it is the level absent from type B, the shared majority-vs-singleton contributes at most seven and the singleton-vs-singleton at most 12, totaling 19.

**Two type B.** If their two used level sets differ, they share only one old level and overlap at most 20. If majority and singleton levels are exchanged, use the seven bound on both, totaling 14. Otherwise they have the same majority and same singleton level.
* If singleton positions coincide and their signed letters differ, overlap in the singleton level is zero and the majority contributes at most 20.
* If positions and signed singleton letters coincide, at least one majority letter differs. The singleton common count is at most eight by a different same-level letter. In the majority layer, if its three underlying axes agree for both candidates, a change of sign means at least one fixed position must be omitted from the two matches, giving at most three old words with two allowable signs each. If exactly one majority axis differs, any common blocker must match the other two axes (four words). Two of these match one of the disparate axes and each has at most one admissible sign choice (the candidate missing it needs positive singleton phase); the two others have at most two choices each. If two majority axes differ, a common blocker must match the shared axis and, on the other two slots, one of the two crossed assignments; the at most two fixed three-axis words each has at most two admissible signs. If all three axes differ, four matches cannot be realized on three slots. Thus the majority common count is at most six in this case.
* If singleton positions differ, the singleton common count is at most four. For the majority, consider the **two common majority positions**. A literal signed match in such a position contributes simultaneously to both candidates only if the two letters there are exactly equal. If neither position can match both, at least four total matches across all four slots are needed, forcing both non-common matches and a distribution of the two common positions one to each candidate. There are at most two fully prescribed signed words. If just one position can match both, it must do so, and a further match for each candidate comes from its non-common slot or the other common slot (the latter cannot serve both). There are at most three sets of three prescribed signed letters, each allowing at most two words. If both common positions can serve both candidates, the cases using exactly one double match require both non-common matches and give at most four words by three-slot Latin counting. The case using both double matches leaves four underlying Latin words. For each, if its axes at the two remaining slots either both or neither match the opposing privileged majority letters, both signs are forced for a blocker, so at most one admissible word. If exactly one axis matches, at most two signs remain; there are at most two such underlying words because each specific third-axis match occurs once. This gives at most six more. Thus their majority overlap is at most ten.

Consequently any distinct expensive pair has at most 20 blockers in common. Each has 36, so **at least \(36+36-20=52\)** distinct old dense points must be removed to insert them. Combined with the first argument, any improvement preserving the full scaffold must make at least this many old dense deletions. The result does not preclude larger trades, other initial stocks, or changes to the scaffold.
