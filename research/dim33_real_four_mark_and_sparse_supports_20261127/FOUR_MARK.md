## Theorem and exact scope

Take the literal Cheng–Sloane [32,17,8] binary C from the `dense_generator` array in KissingNumbers `verifications/improved/dim32-35-signed-layers/nonantipodal33/certificate33_370408_explicit.json` (blob a54ead19f3c683bf2423b6112673583f954b410e). Cheng–Sloane primary source: https://doi.org/10.1137/0402003, author PDF https://neilsloane.com/doc/cheng32.pdf. Mark any 4 of the 32 binary coordinates, and take the shortening C0 to the other 28. Replace all the dense stock by any complete cosets of C0, each furnished with a constant arbitrary real head h of squared norm 4 in the four marked coordinates and the additional 33rd coordinate. Body coordinates remain ±1, so every new point has norm²32. Then **at most 16 complete cosets = 131,072 points** can coexist, even without any sparse, octad, root or star constraints. This strengthens the earlier m=4 result (flat heads, or appending to the original stock) to an arbitrary replacement with arbitrary real heads. It does not exclude partial cosets, point-dependent heads, a different binary parent, five or more marked coordinates, or other geometries.

Use the explicit 15-bit syndrome columns H in `lower99_33d_four_mark_real.cjs`, which have rank 15 and kernel exactly C. For a fixed mark set M, syndromes are computed using the other 28 columns of H; they still span all 15 bits, so shortened C0 has dimension 13. The coset word distance d(s,s') is the exact shortest unweighted walk from 0 to s XOR s' with unmarked columns as generators. Every distance is at most 7, checked over all mark sets. This is also exactly the minimum Hamming distance between the two complete cosets because actual body words of each syndrome are all present. With radius 32, the full cross condition is
`h_s · h_t ≤ 2 d(s,t) − 12`.
A repeated syndrome cannot coexist, since d=0 demands dot≤−12. The parity of any syndrome is well-defined because all C words have even weight, and gives a bipartition of the finite labels.

## Near-orthoplex argument: why only distance seven matters across parity

Within a fixed parity, distances d are even and at most 6, hence the heads have pairwise nonpositive dot. Their Gram is PSD of rank at most 5. Split the Gram matrix by connectivity of strictly negative off-diagonal entries. Every block is either nonsingular or has 1-dimensional nullspace spanned by a strictly positive vector (Perron–Frobenius on a shifted matrix, including for irreducible blocks of size two). A singular block is a positively dependent set, of size at least two; different blocks are orthogonal. Thus there are at most 10 heads per parity, and every set of k heads has at least k−5 distinct singular blocks. In particular, any head on the opposite side has at least k−5 *different* distance-seven neighbors among these labels: if every distance to a singular block were ≤5, each dot with that block would be ≤−2, contradicting its positive dependence. Nonzero same-parity distances ≤2 are impossible, and a same-parity distance of 4 forces an exact antipodal pair. Hence all d=4 edges among selected labels form a matching.

If a set of 17 heads exists, its parity counts after swapping sides are either 9+8 or 10+7. The sharper minimum numbers of distance-seven neighbors needed from each tag on the smaller side are **5 to the 9-set** and **6 to the 10-set**:
- A set of 10 nonpositive heads must consist of five orthogonal antipodal pairs. Every opposite tag g needs at least one d=7 neighbor in each pair, since the two dots are opposite. If there is exactly one neighbor in such a pair, the two dots must be precisely +2 and −2, because the d≤5 bound is −2 and the global d≤7 bound is +2. Exactly five neighbors would make five orthogonal unit-axis components of g have absolute value 1, contradicting norm²4. Thus at least six neighbors.
- A set of 9 consists either of four orthogonal antipodal pairs plus one orthogonal singleton, or three orthogonal antipodal pairs plus one orthogonal singular triple, by counting its at least four singular blocks. In the four-pair case, if each pair gives exactly one distance-seven neighbor, four unit-axis components already exhaust norm²4. The dot with the singleton is then zero, and requires its own d=7 edge.
- In the triple case, the only way to have at most four distance-seven neighbors is exactly one in each of the three antipodal pairs and one in the triple. The three forced unit-axis components of g have squared sum 3, leaving squared norm at most 1 in the triple plane. Each of the two remaining triple heads (both norm 2) must have dot ≤−2 with that projection. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz would then force them equal, impossible since their mutual dot is ≤0. Thus the 9-set case also needs ≥5.

## Small exact residue check

Let S be the set of distance-seven syndromes for the marked set. Every smaller-side label must have ≥5 or ≥6 neighbors among the 9 or 10 larger labels, respectively. In both cases, any two smaller-side labels must share a larger-side neighbor by pigeonhole, so their XOR lies in S+S. Translate one smaller-side label to 0. Every nonzero smaller-side label now lies in S+S, its distance is either 4 or 6, and its d=4 edges on the whole selected set are a matching. A translated same-parity class still has the same metric; labels need not equal the old marked-span quotient.

The standalone exact Node checker computes 384 actual coordinate automorphisms of the literal binary code (backtrack on weight-eight 3-incidence tensor and confirm each on the generator), certifies all 35,960 four-sets covered by their 140 mark orbits, and BFSes all 32,768 syndrome distances in each representative. For each candidate smaller-side tuple O it enumerates all points in its opposite distance-seven neighborhoods. It sums the top 9 or 10 opposite degrees to O, safely ignoring other requirements on that side. For S sizes below 5 there can be no candidate outright. For every orbit except M={0,1,2,5}, the smaller-side pair conditions already reject the candidate. In that exceptional orbit, 1 normalized 8-set and 7 normalized 7-sets survive; all are contained in the 8-element vector space W={0,3,5,6,33,34,36,39}. Opposite degree is at most 4 on each tuple (also directly seen from the checker); the edge sums are at most 36 < 8×5 and 40 < 7×6, respectively. Therefore no 17 banks exist. All arithmetic is integer and there is no solver call or numerical assumption. The original 16 complete cosets attain the cap.
